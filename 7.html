Overview

The core laws and definitions only — no long lecture. Use this as your final revision sheet.

Electrolytes

  • Substances that form ions in solution and conduct electric current.
  • Strong: completely ionized (HCl, Na₂SO₄).
  • Weak: small degree of ionization (ephedrine, phenobarbital).

Classes of Strong Electrolytes

  • Soluble salts — e.g. NaCl
  • Strong acids — HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄
  • Strong bases — NaOH, KOH
  • Weak electrolytes: weak acids (acetic acid), weak bases (NH₄OH)

Electrolysis

  • Cathode: electrons combine with cations.
  • Anode: anions discharge electrons.
  • Reduction = addition of electrons.
  • Oxidation = removal of electrons.

Osmotic Pressure

  • Non-electrolytes: π = RTc
  • Electrolytes need a correction factor i (van't Hoff factor):
  • π = iRTc

The i Factor

  • Non-electrolytes → i ≈ 1
  • Strong electrolytes → i tends toward the number of ions formed.
  • NaCl, CaSO₄ → 2   K₂SO₄, CaCl₂ → 3   FeCl₃ → 4

Interionic Attraction

  • Negligible in dilute solutions, significant at moderate/high concentration.
  • Ions associate into ion pairs (Na⁺Cl⁻) and triplets (Na⁺Cl⁻Na⁺).
  • Result: real i is less than the theoretical i.

Activity & Activity Coefficient

  • Effective concentration = activity (a).
  • At infinite dilution: a = m, ratio a/m = 1.
  • Practical coefficients: a = γₘm, a = γcc
  • Rational coefficient (mole fraction): a = γₓX

Activity of the Solvent

  • Infinitely dilute solution → X₁ = 1 = a
  • Volatile solvent: a₁ = p₁/p°
  • Ratio of vapor pressure in solution to pure solvent.

Ionic Strength (μ)

  • μ = ½ Σ cᵢzᵢ²
  • cᵢ = concentration (mol/L) of an ion, zᵢ = its valence.
  • Total μ of a mixture = sum of each electrolyte's μ.
  • Un-ionized weak acid contributes almost nothing to μ.
